**Repairing a Relationship After You Have Caused Harm**
============================================================================================

When we hurt someone we care about, the rupture can linger in our hearts and disrupt the peace God intends for our communities. Repairing that breach is not just about easing guilt; it is a tangible expression of loving God and loving neighbor as Jesus taught. Approaching reconciliation with humility opens the door to healing, grace, and renewed trust.

## Scripture Foundation
Jesus links worship with reconciliation, reminding us that right relationship with others precedes our offering to God:  
> “If you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there in front of the altar. First go and be reconciled to them; then come and offer your gift.” — Matthew 5:23‑24 (NIV)  
*(Scripture quotations are from the New International Version (NIV).)*  

The prophet Micah calls us to act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly—qualities essential when we seek to make amends:  
> “He has shown you, O mortal, what is good. And what does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God.” — Micah 6:8 (NIV)  
*(Scripture quotations are from the New International Version (NIV).)*  

Paul urges the church to embody kindness and forgiveness, reflecting the grace we have received:  
> “Be kind and compassionate to one another, forgiving each other, just as in Christ God forgave you.” — Ephesians 4:32 (NIV)  
*(Scripture quotations are from the New International Version (NIV).)*  

These passages together guide us: acknowledge the harm, pursue restitution with a humble heart, and extend the mercy we have been given.

## What This Looks Like in Daily Life
- **Pause and listen:** Before defending ourselves, we ask the person we hurt how the action affected them, giving space for their feelings without interruption.  
- **Name the specific wrong:** Rather than a vague “I’m sorry if you were offended,” we say, “I spoke harshly to you yesterday, and I see now that it made you feel disrespected.”  
- **Make restitution when possible:** If we broke trust by sharing confidential information, we might commit to not repeating the breach and, if appropriate, inform the person what steps we’re taking to rebuild confidence.  
- **Seek accountability:** Sharing our intention to repair with a trusted friend, mentor, or small group helps us stay honest and follow through.  
- **Allow time for healing:** We understand that forgiveness may not be immediate; we continue to demonstrate changed behavior over days and weeks.

## Questions for Reflection
1. When I recall a recent hurt I caused, what specific actions or words contributed to the pain?  
2. How might pride or fear be preventing me from initiating a conversation about reconciliation?  
3. In what ways can I demonstrate genuine humility rather than just offering a quick apology?  
4. What practical step can I take to make restitution, even if it feels small?  
5. Who in my life can I trust to hold me accountable as I work toward repair?  
6. How does remembering God’s forgiveness toward me shape my willingness to extend forgiveness to others?

## A Simple Practice This Week
> **Practice:** Choose one relationship where you recognize you have caused harm. Set aside 15 minutes to write a brief, honest note (or prepare a short conversation) that names the specific hurt, expresses regret without excuses, and asks what you can do to make things right.  
> **Gentle next step:** After delivering your note or having the conversation, spend a few minutes in prayer, thanking God for the courage to seek reconciliation and asking for wisdom to listen well to the other person's response.

## Prayer

Fill our hearts with your mercy so that we may extend it to others, reflecting the love of Jesus who reconciles us to you and to one another. In his name we pray, Amen.

## Key Takeaways
- Reconciliation begins with honest acknowledgment of the specific harm done.  
- Humility, mercy, and concrete restitution reflect the character of Christ.  
- Listening to the wounded party is as important as expressing our regret.  
- Healing may take time; consistent changed actions rebuild trust.  
- Involving trusted community members provides accountability and encouragement.
